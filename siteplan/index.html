The Review

Resident Evil Requiem is terrifying in the best way possible. Capcom has taken everything that made the modern RE era great and cranked it to eleven — the tension, the atmosphere, the grotesque creativity of the enemy design. From the first hour, the game grabs you by the throat and does not let go.

Leon and Ashley's dynamic hits different this time around. The writing is sharper, the scares are earned, and the level design is some of the most claustrophobic, brilliant work I've seen in the series. Every corner you turn could be your last — and the game knows exactly how to make you feel that.

Graphically, it's stunning. Lighting, particle effects, the grotesque detail on the enemies — this is what next-gen horror looks like. The sound design deserves a separate award entirely. Play this with headphones. Seriously.

What Works

  • Genuinely terrifying atmosphere from start to finish
  • Incredible enemy design and variety
  • Best-in-class sound design and audio horror
  • Sharp, engaging story with real emotional stakes
  • Brilliant claustrophobic level design

What Doesn't

  • Some mid-game pacing drags slightly
  • A couple of boss fights feel recycled in concept
  • Resource management can feel punishing on first run
The final act twist — which I won't name but you'll know exactly when it hits — is one of the most gut-punch moments in Resident Evil history. The way Requiem ties its narrative back to earlier games while still feeling fresh is a masterclass in franchise continuity. The ending left me genuinely stunned and immediately wanting a sequel.

The Review

Star Wars Battlefront 2 had a rocky launch, but time has been very kind to it. After years of updates and a player-driven resurgence, this is now one of the best Star Wars games ever made — a bold claim, but a defensible one.

The galactic assault maps are massive and chaotic in the best way. Dropping into Kashyyyk, Geonosis, or Felucia with a full 40-player server feels genuinely cinematic. The hero system, once a mess, is now a satisfying reward loop. Playing as Darth Maul and sliding through a crowd of enemies never, ever gets old.

Visually it still holds up beautifully. The attention to detail in the environments — the way Clone Trooper armor catches the light, the scale of the capital ships — shows how much love went into this game's world-building. It might be older, but it still slaps harder than most modern shooters.

What Works

  • Massive, cinematic multiplayer battles
  • Incredible roster of heroes and villains
  • Stunning visual fidelity for its age
  • Galactic Assault is endlessly replayable
  • Excellent Star Wars atmosphere and sound

What Doesn't

  • Smaller player counts now on some servers
  • Single-player campaign is short and forgettable
  • No new content coming — it's frozen in time
Iden Versio's campaign arc is genuinely compelling as a rare story told from the Imperial perspective — her eventual defection is handled with more nuance than the short runtime suggests. The Jakku sequence in particular is spectacular and feels like a proper movie moment. Wish the campaign was twice as long.

The Review

I went in expecting a great RPG. I was not prepared for what Expedition 33 actually was. This game reached into my chest and rearranged things. The turn-based combat is slick and genuinely innovative — the parry and dodge timing system adds a kinetic layer to every single fight — but the combat isn't what stays with you.

It's the story. The world of Expedition 33 is painted in grief, in loss, in the peculiar bravery of people who walk toward extinction with their eyes open. The characters feel devastatingly real. Gustave, Maelle, Lune — I cared about all of them more than I have for fictional characters in years.

The art direction is staggering. French Belle Époque aesthetics filtered through dark fantasy produce something entirely unlike anything else in games right now. The soundtrack is haunting. There were moments where I had to put the controller down and just sit with what had happened on screen. I didn't know I had emotions. Expedition 33 found them.

What Works

  • Emotionally devastating, beautifully written story
  • Innovative real-time parry / dodge in turn-based combat
  • Stunning, completely unique art direction
  • Characters you genuinely love and fear for
  • Haunting, unforgettable soundtrack

What Doesn't

  • Some early boss difficulty spikes feel unfair
  • Pacing in Act 2 dips briefly before recovering
  • A few side quests feel disconnected from the main tone
The revelation about the Paintress — and who she actually is — recontextualizes the entire journey in a way that is simultaneously heartbreaking and beautiful. The final choice the game presents you with is one of the most morally complex decisions I've ever faced in an RPG. I've thought about it every day since the credits rolled.

The Review

Crimson Desert is the kind of open world that makes you forget you had plans. Pearl Abyss has built something genuinely ambitious here — a massive, gorgeous, lived-in world that begs to be explored in every direction. You want to sail? Sail. You want to climb that impossible mountain? Go. You want to catch a dragon? There's a dragon.

The combat system is fluid and brutal and deeply satisfying once it clicks. The main character Macduff is compelling — a scarred, complicated mercenary whose story unfolds across a continent that feels historically real. Side content ranges from excellent to genuinely surprising in its depth.

The set-pieces are jaw-dropping. Crimson Desert understands spectacle. Naval battles, aerial sequences, fortress sieges — the game swings for the fences constantly and connects more often than not. Set sail, fly high. This world rewards the curious and the bold.

What Works

  • Stunning, enormous open world full of secrets
  • Spectacular, cinematic set-pieces throughout
  • Fluid, satisfying combat that rewards skill
  • Naval and aerial gameplay adds real variety
  • Macduff is a genuinely compelling lead

What Doesn't

  • Story occasionally loses focus in mid-game
  • Some open-world padding dilutes the experience
  • Technical performance can be inconsistent
The revelation of Macduff's true lineage — and how it connects to the Crimson Desert itself — is a genuinely exciting payoff for players who stuck with the main questline. The final confrontation atop the Obsidian Spire is one of the most visually spectacular boss encounters in recent memory. The ending sets up a sequel hook that I'm genuinely excited for.
